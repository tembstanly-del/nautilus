# Lesson 3: Liquid Dosage Forms - Solutions and Suspensions

## Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:
1. Distinguish between solutions, suspensions, and emulsions
2. Understand the principles of solubility and dissolution
3. Select appropriate vehicles and additives for liquid preparations
4. Prepare pharmaceutical solutions using proper techniques
5. Compound stable suspensions using appropriate methods
6. Calculate beyond-use dates for liquid preparations
7. Apply quality control measures to liquid dosage forms

## Overview of Liquid Dosage Forms

Liquid dosage forms are among the most common compounded preparations. They offer several advantages:

### Advantages
- **Ease of administration** - Especially for children and elderly
- **Dose flexibility** - Easy to adjust dosage
- **Rapid absorption** - Faster onset than solid dosage forms
- **Alternative for dysphagia** - Patients unable to swallow solids

### Disadvantages
- **Stability concerns** - Shorter shelf life
- **Bulky** - Less convenient to carry
- **Taste challenges** - May require flavoring
- **Accuracy concerns** - Measurement errors possible

## Solutions

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances at the molecular level.

### Components of Solutions

1. **Solute** - The substance being dissolved (drug)
2. **Solvent** - The substance doing the dissolving (usually water)
3. **Solution** - The resulting homogeneous mixture

### Types of Solutions

#### Based on Solvent:
- **Aqueous solutions** - Water is the solvent
- **Alcoholic solutions** - Alcohol is the solvent (e.g., tinctures, spirits)
- **Hydroalcoholic solutions** - Mixture of water and alcohol (e.g., elixirs)
- **Non-aqueous solutions** - Other solvents (oils, propylene glycol)

#### Special Types:
- **Syrups** - Aqueous solutions with high sugar content (≥45% sucrose)
- **Elixirs** - Sweetened hydroalcoholic solutions
- **Tinctures** - Alcoholic or hydroalcoholic extracts of plant/animal material
- **Spirits** - Alcoholic solutions of volatile substances

## Principles of Solubility

### Factors Affecting Solubility

1. **Temperature**
   - Most solids: solubility increases with temperature
   - Gases: solubility decreases with temperature
   - Heat may be used to aid dissolution

2. **pH**
   - Weak acids more soluble in alkaline pH
   - Weak bases more soluble in acidic pH
   - May need pH adjustment

3. **Particle Size**
   - Smaller particles dissolve faster
   - Greater surface area for dissolution
   - May need to reduce particle size (comminution)

4. **Agitation**
   - Stirring increases dissolution rate
   - Brings fresh solvent to solute surface

5. **Nature of Solute and Solvent**
   - "Like dissolves like"
   - Polar substances dissolve in polar solvents
   - Non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents

### The "Like Dissolves Like" Rule

- **Polar solutes** (e.g., salts, sugars) → **Polar solvents** (e.g., water)
- **Non-polar solutes** (e.g., oils, fats) → **Non-polar solvents** (e.g., mineral oil, chloroform)

## Compounding Pharmaceutical Solutions

### General Procedure

1. **Calculate quantities** of all ingredients
2. **Gather and verify** all materials and equipment
3. **Reduce particle size** if needed (powder drugs in mortar)
4. **Dissolve solute** in portion of solvent with mixing
5. **Add additional ingredients** (preservatives, buffers, flavorings)
6. **Adjust pH** if necessary
7. **Bring to final volume** with solvent (qs to final volume)
8. **Mix thoroughly**
9. **Quality control** (visual inspection, pH, clarity)
10. **Package and label** appropriately

### Example: Preparing a Simple Solution

**Prescription:**
```
Sodium Chloride Solution 0.9% (Normal Saline)
Make 500 mL
```

**Calculation:**
```
500 mL × (0.9 g NaCl / 100 mL) = 4.5 g NaCl
```

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh 4.5 g of sodium chloride USP
2. Transfer to beaker
3. Add approximately 400 mL purified water
4. Stir until completely dissolved
5. Transfer to graduated cylinder
6. Add purified water qs to 500 mL
7. Mix well
8. Check clarity and pH
9. Package in appropriate bottle
10. Label with instructions and beyond-use date

### Order of Mixing

**General rule:** Dissolve ingredients in order of:
1. Substances requiring heat (if any)
2. Preservatives
3. Active ingredients
4. Buffers and pH adjusters
5. Sweeteners
6. Flavorings and colorings (last)

## Suspensions

A **suspension** is a dispersion of finely divided, undissolved drug particles in a liquid vehicle.

### Characteristics of Suspensions
- **Heterogeneous** - Not uniform at molecular level
- **Unstable** - Particles tend to settle or aggregate
- **Require shaking** - "Shake Well" label required
- **Opaque appearance** - Due to suspended particles

### When to Use Suspensions

1. **Drug is insoluble** in available solvents
2. **Improved stability** - Some drugs more stable as suspension
3. **Improved taste** - Insoluble form may taste better
4. **Controlled release** - Slower dissolution = prolonged action
5. **High drug concentration** needed

### Theory of Suspensions

#### Settling Rate (Stokes' Law)

Particles in suspension settle according to Stokes' Law:

```
v = [d² × (ρₚ - ρₘ) × g] / (18 × η)
```

Where:
- v = settling velocity
- d = particle diameter
- ρₚ = particle density
- ρₘ = medium density
- g = gravitational constant
- η = viscosity of medium

**Practical implications:**
- **Smaller particles** settle slower (reduce particle size)
- **Higher viscosity** slows settling (add suspending agent)
- **Similar densities** slow settling (adjust vehicle density)

### Suspending Agents

Suspending agents increase viscosity and slow particle settling:

#### Natural Polymers:
- **Acacia (Gum Arabic)** - 10-20% concentration
- **Tragacanth** - 1-2% concentration
- **Sodium alginate** - 1-5% concentration

#### Semisynthetic Cellulose Derivatives:
- **Methylcellulose (MC)** - 1-2% concentration
- **Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC)** - 0.5-2% concentration
- **Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC)** - 1-5% concentration

#### Synthetic Polymers:
- **Carbomer (Carbopol)** - 0.5-2% concentration
- **Povidone (PVP)** - 2-10% concentration

#### Clays:
- **Bentonite** - 2-5% concentration (requires activation with water)
- **Veegum** - 2-5% concentration

### Preparing Suspensions

#### Method 1: Levigation
Use when starting with a powder

**Procedure:**
1. Place powder in mortar
2. Add small amount of **levigating agent** (glycerin, propylene glycol, or small amount of vehicle)
3. Triturate to form smooth paste
4. Add vehicle in portions with continued trituration
5. Transfer to graduate
6. Rinse mortar with vehicle and add to graduate
7. Qs to final volume with vehicle
8. Package and label (include "Shake Well")

**Example levigating agents:**
- Glycerin (for aqueous vehicles)
- Mineral oil (for oil-based vehicles)
- Propylene glycol
- Small amount of final vehicle

#### Method 2: From Tablets or Capsules

**Procedure:**
1. Count required number of tablets/capsules
2. Crush tablets or empty capsules in mortar
3. Add small amount of vehicle to form paste
4. Triturate thoroughly
5. Add vehicle in portions with mixing
6. Transfer to graduate
7. Rinse mortar and add to graduate
8. Qs to final volume
9. Package and label (include "Shake Well")

**Important:** Verify no extended-release coating or mechanism that should not be crushed

### Structured vs. Flocculated Suspensions

#### Deflocculated (Structured) Suspensions:
- **Appearance:** Individual particles, smooth texture
- **Settling:** Slow, forms tight sediment
- **Redispersion:** Difficult (caking problem)
- **Elegant appearance**

#### Flocculated Suspensions:
- **Appearance:** Clumped particles, grainy texture
- **Settling:** Faster, forms loose sediment
- **Redispersion:** Easy
- **Less elegant but more practical**

**Goal:** Controlled flocculation for easy redispersion

### Wetting Agents

For hydrophobic drugs that don't mix well with water:

- **Surfactants** reduce surface tension
- Allow water to penetrate powder
- Examples: polysorbate 80, sodium lauryl sulfate, polyethylene glycol

**Procedure:**
1. Mix powder with wetting agent
2. Allow to stand (wet thoroughly)
3. Add vehicle with mixing

## Additives in Liquid Preparations

### Preservatives
Protect against microbial contamination:
- **Methylparaben** 0.05-0.25% (better against molds/yeasts)
- **Propylparaben** 0.01-0.06% (better against bacteria)
- **Combination:** e.g., methylparaben 0.18% + propylparaben 0.02%
- **Benzoic acid** 0.1-0.5%
- **Sorbic acid** 0.05-0.2%
- **Benzalkonium chloride** 0.002-0.02%

**Not needed if:**
- High alcohol content (>15-20%)
- High sugar content (syrups)
- For immediate use
- Intrinsically antimicrobial (e.g., strong acids/bases)

### Antioxidants
Prevent oxidative degradation:
- **Ascorbic acid** 0.05-0.1%
- **Sodium bisulfite** 0.1-0.15%
- **Sodium metabisulfite** 0.1-1%
- **Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA)** 0.001-0.02%
- **Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT)** 0.001-0.02%

### Buffers
Maintain stable pH:
- **Citrate buffer** (pH 3-6)
- **Acetate buffer** (pH 4-6)
- **Phosphate buffer** (pH 6-8)
- **Carbonate buffer** (pH 9-11)

### Sweeteners

#### Nutritive:
- **Sucrose** (most common in syrups)
- **Sorbitol** 30-70% (for diabetics)
- **Glycerin** 20-50%

#### Non-nutritive:
- **Saccharin sodium** 0.02-0.5%
- **Aspartame** (not for PKU patients)
- **Sucralose** 0.016-0.03%

### Flavorings

**Principles:**
- Match flavor to drug taste
- Salty/bitter → fruit flavors (cherry, raspberry, orange)
- Sour → citrus or berry flavors
- Oil-based drugs → spice flavors (mint, cinnamon)

**Types:**
- Natural extracts
- Synthetic flavors
- Essential oils

**Concentration:** Usually 0.1-0.5%

## Quality Control for Liquid Preparations

### Visual Inspection

**For solutions:**
- Should be clear (may shine light through)
- Free from particles
- Uniform color
- No cloudiness

**For suspensions:**
- Uniform appearance after shaking
- No caking on bottom
- Appropriate viscosity
- No separation of vehicle

### pH Testing
- Use calibrated pH meter or pH paper
- Document pH value
- Should be within expected range for formulation
- Important for stability and comfort

### Volume Check
- Use graduated cylinder
- Verify final volume is correct
- Important for accurate dosing

### Beyond-Use Dating

Per USP <795> for water-containing preparations:
- **14 days** when stored at controlled cold temperature (2-8°C)
- May be longer if stability data available
- Must consider:
  - Drug stability
  - Preservative efficacy
  - Container/closure
  - Storage conditions

## Common Examples

### Example 1: Oral Antibiotic Suspension

**Prescription:**
```
Amoxicillin 50 mg/mL suspension
Make 100 mL
Sig: Take 5 mL twice daily
```

**Calculation:**
- Need: 100 mL × 50 mg/mL = 5000 mg amoxicillin
- Available: 500 mg capsules
- Number of capsules: 5000 mg / 500 mg = 10 capsules

**Procedure:**
1. Empty 10 amoxicillin capsules into mortar
2. Add small amount of Ora-Sweet to form paste
3. Triturate well
4. Add Ora-Sweet in portions with mixing
5. Transfer to graduate
6. Rinse mortar with Ora-Sweet
7. Qs to 100 mL with Ora-Sweet
8. Package in amber bottle
9. Label: "Shake well before use. Keep refrigerated. Discard after 14 days."

### Example 2: Flavored Potassium Chloride Solution

**Prescription:**
```
Potassium Chloride 10% solution, flavored
Make 120 mL
```

**Formulation:**
- Potassium chloride: 12 g
- Cherry flavor: 0.2 mL
- Sucralose: 0.05 g
- Purified water qs to 120 mL

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh 12 g potassium chloride
2. Dissolve in approximately 100 mL water
3. Add sucralose and dissolve
4. Add cherry flavor
5. Qs to 120 mL with water
6. Mix thoroughly
7. Check pH
8. Package and label

## Packaging Considerations

### Container Selection

**Solutions:**
- Tight, light-resistant (amber) bottles if light-sensitive
- Dropper bottles for ophthalmic/otic solutions
- Oral syringes for accurate pediatric dosing

**Suspensions:**
- Wide-mouth bottles (easier to shake and pour)
- Amber bottles if light-sensitive
- Adequate headspace for shaking

### Closure Types
- **Child-resistant** (required for most prescriptions)
- **Non-child-resistant** (if requested or patient unable to use CRC)
- **Dropper caps** for drops
- **Oral syringes** for accurate dosing

## Key Takeaways

1. Solutions are homogeneous; suspensions are heterogeneous
2. "Like dissolves like" - consider polarity when selecting solvents
3. Suspensions require suspending agents and always need "Shake Well" labels
4. Levigation is key technique for creating smooth suspensions from powders
5. Preservatives needed for water-containing preparations (unless exempted)
6. Beyond-use dating for water-containing preparations: 14 days refrigerated (per USP <795>)
7. Quality control includes visual inspection, pH testing, and volume verification
8. Proper flavoring improves patient compliance, especially for pediatric patients

## Practice Problem

**Prescription:**
```
Ranitidine 15 mg/mL suspension
Make 60 mL
```

Available: Ranitidine 150 mg tablets

Calculate:
1. How many mg of ranitidine are needed?
2. How many tablets are required?
3. Describe the compounding procedure

**Answer:**
1. 60 mL × 15 mg/mL = 900 mg ranitidine
2. 900 mg / 150 mg per tablet = 6 tablets
3. Crush 6 tablets in mortar, leviga with small amount of Ora-Plus, add Ora-Sweet in portions, qs to 60 mL, package in amber bottle, label "Shake Well. Refrigerate. Discard after 14 days."
