5. Verify calculation results using reasonableness checks

## The Conceptual Approach to Pharmaceutical Calculations

Rather than memorizing formulas, the **conceptual approach** emphasizes:
- Understanding the underlying principles
- Using dimensional analysis
- Thinking through problems logically
- Checking for reasonableness
- Applying clinical judgment

### Why a Conceptual Approach?

**Traditional approach:** Memorize formulas → Plug in numbers → Hope for the right answer

**Conceptual approach:** Understand the problem → Think through the logic → Use dimensional analysis → Verify the answer makes sense

## Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis (also called unit factor method or factor-label method) is a problem-solving method that uses conversion factors to convert from one unit to another.

### Key Principles

1. **Units must match** - You can only add or subtract like units
2. **Conversion factors** - Equal quantities in different units (e.g., 1 kg = 1000 g)
3. **Units cancel** - Arrange conversion factors so unwanted units cancel out
4. **Final units** - The answer should have the units you need

### Example: Basic Conversion

**Problem:** Convert 150 pounds to kilograms (1 kg = 2.2 lb)

**Solution:**
```
150 lb × (1 kg / 2.2 lb) = 68.18 kg
```

Notice how the "lb" units cancel out, leaving "kg"

## Dosage Calculations

### Basic Dosage Calculation

**Problem:** A patient needs 20 mg of medication. The available tablets are 10 mg each. How many tablets should be given?

**Conceptual thinking:**
- I need 20 mg
- Each tablet provides 10 mg
- So I need 20 ÷ 10 = 2 tablets

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
20 mg × (1 tablet / 10 mg) = 2 tablets
```

### Weight-Based Dosing

**Problem:** A medication is dosed at 5 mg/kg. The patient weighs 70 kg. What is the dose?

**Conceptual thinking:**
- For each kilogram, give 5 mg
- Patient weighs 70 kg
- Total dose = 5 mg/kg × 70 kg = 350 mg

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
70 kg × (5 mg / 1 kg) = 350 mg
```

### Pediatric Dosing Example

**Problem:** A child weighs 15 kg and needs amoxicillin at 40 mg/kg/day divided into 3 doses. Calculate:
a) Total daily dose
b) Dose per administration

**Solution:**

a) Total daily dose:
```
15 kg × (40 mg / kg / day) = 600 mg/day
```

b) Dose per administration:
```
600 mg/day × (1 day / 3 doses) = 200 mg per dose
```

**Reasonableness check:**
- 15 kg child is roughly a toddler
- 200 mg three times daily seems reasonable for amoxicillin
- Not too high (which could be toxic) or too low (which would be ineffective)

## Concentration Calculations

### Understanding Concentration

**Concentration** expresses the amount of drug in a given quantity of product.

Common expressions:
- mg/mL (mass per volume)
- g/L (grams per liter)
- % (percentage strength)
- Ratio (e.g., 1:1000)

### Calculating Quantity from Concentration

**Problem:** How many milliliters of a 10 mg/mL solution are needed to provide 75 mg?

**Conceptual thinking:**
- Each mL contains 10 mg
- I need 75 mg
- So I need 75 ÷ 10 = 7.5 mL

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
75 mg × (1 mL / 10 mg) = 7.5 mL
```

### Calculating Amount of Drug

**Problem:** How many milligrams are in 5 mL of a 25 mg/mL solution?

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
5 mL × (25 mg / 1 mL) = 125 mg
```

## Percentage Strength

Percentage strength can be expressed three ways:

1. **% w/w (weight-in-weight)**: grams of ingredient in 100 g of product
2. **% w/v (weight-in-volume)**: grams of ingredient in 100 mL of product
3. **% v/v (volume-in-volume)**: mL of ingredient in 100 mL of product

### Default Convention
- **Solids in liquids**: % w/v
- **Liquids in liquids**: % v/v
- **Solids in solids**: % w/w

### Percentage Calculations

**Problem:** How many grams of hydrocortisone are in 30 g of 2.5% hydrocortisone cream?

**Conceptual thinking:**
- 2.5% means 2.5 g per 100 g
- I have 30 g of cream
- Amount = (2.5/100) × 30 = 0.75 g

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
30 g cream × (2.5 g hydrocortisone / 100 g cream) = 0.75 g hydrocortisone
```

### Creating a Percentage Solution

**Problem:** Prepare 200 mL of a 5% w/v solution of drug X.

**Conceptual thinking:**
- 5% w/v means 5 g in 100 mL
- I need 200 mL (which is 2 × 100 mL)
- So I need 2 × 5 g = 10 g

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
200 mL × (5 g / 100 mL) = 10 g
```

**Procedure:** Weigh 10 g of drug X, dissolve in sufficient water to make 200 mL

## Ratio Strength

Ratio strength expresses concentration as 1 part in X parts (e.g., 1:1000)

### Converting Between Ratio and Percentage

**1:1000 means:**
- 1 g in 1000 mL (for w/v)
- 1 g in 1000 g (for w/w)
- 1 mL in 1000 mL (for v/v)

**To convert ratio to percentage:**
```
1:1000 = 1 g / 1000 mL = 0.1 g / 100 mL = 0.1% w/v
```

**To convert percentage to ratio:**
```
0.5% = 0.5 g / 100 mL = 1 g / 200 mL = 1:200
```

### Ratio Strength Problem

**Problem:** How many grams of active ingredient are in 500 mL of a 1:2000 w/v solution?

**Conceptual thinking:**
- 1:2000 means 1 g in 2000 mL
- I have 500 mL (which is 1/4 of 2000 mL)
- So I have 1/4 of 1 g = 0.25 g

**Dimensional analysis:**
```
500 mL × (1 g / 2000 mL) = 0.25 g
```

## Dilution Calculations

The fundamental principle: **The amount of active ingredient remains constant**

### The Dilution Equation

```
C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂
```

Where:
- C₁ = initial concentration
- V₁ = initial volume
- C₂ = final concentration
- V₂ = final volume

### Dilution Problem

**Problem:** You have 50 mL of a 20% solution. You want to dilute it to a 5% solution. What is the final volume?

**Using the equation:**
```
C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂
20% × 50 mL = 5% × V₂
1000 = 5% × V₂
V₂ = 1000 / 5 = 200 mL
```

**Interpretation:** The final volume is 200 mL, so you need to add 150 mL of diluent

### Conceptual Understanding of Dilution

When you dilute:
- Amount of active ingredient stays the same
- Total volume increases
- Concentration decreases

**Example:**
- Original: 10 g in 100 mL = 10% = 0.1 g/mL
- Add 100 mL water: 10 g in 200 mL = 5% = 0.05 g/mL
- Same 10 g, but more dilute

## Alligation

**Alligation** is a method for calculating the quantities of two different concentrations needed to prepare a desired concentration.

### The Alligation Grid

```
        Higher %
           ↘
              Desired % ─── Parts of higher %
           ↗
        Lower %  ───────── Parts of lower %
```

### Alligation Problem

**Problem:** Prepare 100 g of 5% ointment using 10% and 2% ointments. How much of each is needed?

**Step 1:** Set up the alligation grid
```
10% (higher) ────────┐
                     │→ 5% (desired)
2% (lower) ──────────┘
```

**Step 2:** Calculate differences
```
10% ───── (5 - 2) = 3 parts of 10%
      5%
2% ────── (10 - 5) = 5 parts of 2%
```

**Step 3:** Calculate total parts
```
Total parts = 3 + 5 = 8 parts
```

**Step 4:** Calculate quantities
```
10% ointment: (3/8) × 100 g = 37.5 g
2% ointment: (5/8) × 100 g = 62.5 g
```

**Verification:**
```
(37.5 × 10%) + (62.5 × 2%) = 3.75 + 1.25 = 5.0 g active in 100 g = 5% ✓
```

## Common Conversions to Memorize

### Weight
- 1 kg = 1000 g = 2.2 lb
- 1 g = 1000 mg
- 1 mg = 1000 mcg (or μg)

### Volume
- 1 L = 1000 mL
- 1 tsp (teaspoon) = 5 mL
- 1 tbsp (tablespoon) = 15 mL
- 1 fl oz = 30 mL (approximately)

### Temperature
- °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32
- °C = (°F - 32) × 5/9

## Avoiding Calculation Errors

### Common Errors
1. **Decimal point errors** - Most dangerous!
   - 10 mg vs 100 mg is a 10-fold error
2. **Unit confusion** - mg vs g, mL vs L
3. **Rounding too early** - Keep extra digits until final answer
4. **Wrong formula** - Not understanding the concept
5. **Transcription errors** - Copying numbers incorrectly

### Error Prevention Strategies

1. **Write out all steps clearly**
2. **Include units in every step**
3. **Use dimensional analysis**
4. **Double-check your math**
5. **Perform reasonableness check**
6. **Have someone else verify critical calculations**

### The Reasonableness Check

Always ask yourself:
- Does this answer make sense?
- Is it within the expected range?
- Would this dose be safe for the patient?
- Are the units correct?

**Example:** If you calculate a dose of 50,000 mg for a child, that should trigger immediate review - it's likely an error!

## Practice Problems

### Problem 1: Basic Dosage
A patient needs 15 mg of drug. Available: 5 mg tablets. How many tablets?

### Problem 2: Weight-Based Dosing
Calculate the dose for a 65 kg adult if the medication is dosed at 10 mg/kg/day.

### Problem 3: Concentration
How many mL of a 40 mg/mL solution are needed to provide 180 mg?

### Problem 4: Percentage Strength
How many grams of drug are needed to prepare 250 g of 3% ointment?

### Problem 5: Dilution
You have 20 mL of 50% solution. What is the final volume if diluted to 10%?

## Solutions to Practice Problems

### Solution 1
```
15 mg × (1 tablet / 5 mg) = 3 tablets
```

### Solution 2
```
65 kg × (10 mg / kg / day) = 650 mg/day
```

### Solution 3
```
180 mg × (1 mL / 40 mg) = 4.5 mL
```

### Solution 4
```
250 g × (3 g drug / 100 g ointment) = 7.5 g drug
```

### Solution 5
```
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
50% × 20 mL = 10% × V₂
V₂ = 100 mL (add 80 mL diluent)
```

## Key Takeaways

1. Understanding concepts is more important than memorizing formulas
2. Dimensional analysis is a powerful tool for pharmaceutical calculations
3. Always include units in every step of calculations
4. Perform reasonableness checks on all answers
5. Double-check critical calculations
6. Be especially careful with decimal points
7. Concentration can be expressed multiple ways (%, ratio, mg/mL, etc.)
8. The amount of active ingredient remains constant during dilution
